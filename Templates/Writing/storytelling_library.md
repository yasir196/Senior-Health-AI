# Storytelling Library: Illustrative Stories for Senior Health Scripts

Stories are used to make evidence easier to understand. They do not replace evidence. They must never create fake proof, invented patients, invented emails, or implied clinical experience.

## Story Rules

1. Use illustrative language only.
2. Start clearly with phrases like "Imagine," "Picture," or "Think about."
3. Do not say "my patient," "a patient wrote to me," "in my clinic," or "I treated someone."
4. Do not invent medical outcomes.
5. Do not imply the story proves the claim.
6. Keep stories short: usually 3 to 7 sentences.
7. Use the story to clarify a decision, not to create fear.
8. Return to the evidence or safety boundary immediately after the story.

## Safe Story Openers

- "Imagine a 72-year-old..."
- "Picture someone standing in the store aisle..."
- "Think about a retired teacher..."
- "Picture two neighbors..."
- "Imagine someone making breakfast..."
- "Think about a person who wants to do the right thing but sees five different labels..."
- "Picture a normal Tuesday morning..."
- "Imagine opening the refrigerator and trying to choose the healthier option..."

## Framework 1: Two Neighbors

Use when comparing two similar habits with different reasons.

Structure:

1. "Picture two neighbors, both in their seventies."
2. Neighbor A uses the habit for a practical reason.
3. Neighbor B expects a dramatic medical result.
4. Explain why one expectation is safer than the other.
5. Return to the evidence.

Example:

"Picture two neighbors, both 72. One adds lemon to water because it helps her drink more. The other adds lemon because a video promised younger-looking skin. The first reason is practical. The second reason needs stronger proof."

## Framework 2: Store Aisle Decision

Use when explaining labels, supplements, or product choices.

Structure:

1. Viewer stands in a store aisle.
2. Several products make similar promises.
3. The viewer learns to ask one practical question.
4. The decision becomes clearer.

Example:

"Picture someone standing in the store aisle with three powders in front of them. One says hydration. One says beauty. One says collagen. The smarter question is not which label sounds best. The smarter question is which one fits their health situation."

## Framework 3: Morning Routine

Use when turning evidence into a daily habit.

Structure:

1. Imagine the viewer in the morning.
2. Show the habit in a realistic setting.
3. Add one decision point.
4. Keep the advice simple.

Example:

"Imagine tomorrow morning. You pour a glass of water, and before adding anything, you ask, 'Why am I adding this?' If the answer is taste, keep it simple. If the answer is a supplement effect, slow down and check whether the claim is realistic."

## Framework 4: The Helpful But Limited Tool

Use when a habit has evidence but is commonly overclaimed.

Structure:

1. Introduce the tool.
2. Show where it can help.
3. Show where it cannot help.
4. Give the safe wording.

Example:

"Think of this like a helpful tool in a drawer. It can do one job well, but it cannot repair the whole house. That is how I want you to think about this habit."

## Framework 5: The Label Versus the Body

Use when safety depends on medical conditions or medications.

Structure:

1. The label sounds healthy.
2. The body has a condition that changes the decision.
3. The viewer learns to check before using it daily.

Example:

"A label can look clean and still be wrong for someone with kidney restrictions. The label does not know your lab results, your medications, or your clinician's advice."

## Framework 6: The Practical Swap

Use when replacing a less helpful habit with a safer one.

Structure:

1. Identify a common habit.
2. Offer a realistic swap.
3. Explain the benefit without exaggeration.
4. Keep the viewer encouraged.

Example:

"Picture someone who drinks sweet tea every afternoon. Switching one glass to unsweetened tea may not be a miracle, but it is a practical improvement."

## Framework 7: The Question Test

Use when reducing hype.

Structure:

1. Present the viewer with a simple question.
2. If yes, explain the safe path.
3. If no, explain the caution.

Example:

"Ask yourself: would I still do this if the label did not promise a dramatic result? If yes, it may be a useful habit. If no, the marketing may be doing most of the work."

## Framework 8: The Gentle Caution

Use when warning without scaring.

Structure:

1. Reassure the viewer.
2. Name the group that needs caution.
3. Explain why checking first is responsible.
4. Continue without fear.

Example:

"This is not dangerous for everyone. But if you have kidney disease, heart failure, or medication restrictions, this is the moment to ask first."

## How Stories Reinforce Evidence

Stories should help viewers remember:

- The claim size.
- The safety boundary.
- The practical decision.
- The difference between support and treatment.

Stories should never:

- Introduce a new medical claim.
- Replace a citation or evidence summary.
- Suggest guaranteed results.
- Create a fake patient outcome.
- Make the viewer afraid of ordinary choices.
